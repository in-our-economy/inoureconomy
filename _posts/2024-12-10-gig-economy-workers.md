The rise of app-based platforms — Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart — reshaped how millions of Americans work. It also created a legal gray zone that companies have exploited aggressively: by classifying workers as independent contractors rather than employees, platforms avoided minimum wage requirements, overtime rules, unemployment insurance, workers' compensation, and the employer side of payroll taxes.

For a decade, this arrangement persisted largely unchallenged. That may be changing.

## The Classification Question

The distinction between an employee and an independent contractor is not simply a legal formality. It determines whether a worker receives the full set of protections built into the New Deal and postwar labor law framework. Employees are entitled to minimum wages, overtime pay, the right to organize, protection against workplace discrimination, and access to the social insurance system. Independent contractors get none of these by default.

The traditional legal tests for worker classification — focused on behavioral control, financial control, and the nature of the relationship — were developed for a different era. They weren't designed to adjudicate the status of workers who use an app to connect with customers, set their own hours within algorithmic constraints, and are subject to deactivation at any time.

## The Economic Stakes

The misclassification of gig workers transfers substantial costs from employers to workers and the public. Workers bear the full cost of payroll taxes, must purchase their own health insurance, receive no employer contributions to retirement savings, and have no safety net if they are injured or their income drops.

A 2020 analysis estimated that Uber alone saves hundreds of millions of dollars annually by classifying drivers as contractors. These savings come directly at the expense of drivers and — because misclassified workers often qualify for public benefits — are partially subsidized by taxpayers.

## The Legal Landscape

Several states have moved to tighten worker classification standards. California's AB5, passed in 2019, adopted a strict "ABC test" that presumes workers are employees unless companies can demonstrate otherwise. The law was immediately challenged and, for app-based workers, largely reversed by Proposition 22 — a ballot initiative backed by $200 million in platform spending.

The federal Department of Labor has also been active, issuing new guidance that narrows the definition of independent contractor under the Fair Labor Standards Act. These rules face legal challenges and may not survive political transitions.

The fundamental problem is that the platforms have enormous financial incentives to lobby against reclassification, while individual gig workers have limited resources to advocate for themselves. The political economy strongly favors the status quo.

## What Reform Would Require

A genuine solution to the gig worker problem requires more than clarifying classification rules. It requires adapting labor protections for a world of non-standard work arrangements.

Several proposals are on the table: portable benefits systems that travel with workers rather than attaching to specific employers; sectoral bargaining that sets industry-wide wage and benefit standards regardless of employment status; and new worker organizations that can represent gig workers in negotiations with platforms.

None of these solutions is simple or costless. But the current arrangement — in which the risks of economic disruption are systematically shifted onto the workers least equipped to bear them — is a policy choice, not an inevitability.
