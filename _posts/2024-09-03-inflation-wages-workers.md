The inflationary episode of 2021–2023 generated fierce debate about its causes, consequences, and cures. Lost in much of that debate was a careful accounting of what high inflation actually meant for workers' living standards — and which workers were affected most.

The story is more complicated than either "inflation is a tax on workers" or "wage growth kept pace." The distribution of gains and losses varied substantially across the income spectrum, and some of the conventional wisdom got the direction of effects wrong.

## The Aggregate Story

From early 2021 through mid-2022, nominal wages rose rapidly — faster than at any point in decades. This reflected tight labor markets, record job openings, and what some economists called the "Great Resignation" giving workers unusual bargaining power.

But inflation rose even faster. Measured by the Consumer Price Index, prices rose more than 9% in the year through June 2022. For most workers, nominal wage gains were swamped by price increases, and real wages — wages adjusted for inflation — fell.

By 2023, the picture began to improve. Inflation decelerated sharply, while nominal wage growth remained elevated. Real wages started rising again, and by the end of 2023, some measures of real wages had recovered their pre-pandemic levels.

## Who Won and Who Lost

The aggregate story obscures important distributional differences. Research by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta and the Economic Policy Institute found that real wage gains were concentrated among lower-wage workers, particularly during the 2020-2022 period.

Several factors drove this pattern. Low-wage industries — hospitality, food service, retail — faced severe labor shortages and had to raise wages sharply to attract workers. The tight labor market gave workers at the bottom more bargaining power than they had seen in decades. And because lower-income households spend a higher share of their budgets on food and energy, they were hit harder by inflation in those categories — but the wage gains in low-wage sectors were large enough to partially offset this.

Higher-income workers, by contrast, often saw smaller nominal wage gains (expressed as a percentage) but faced somewhat lower inflation rates because their consumption baskets are different. The net effect is that real wage inequality actually narrowed during this period, even as most workers experienced real wage declines in the aggregate.

## The Measurement Debates

How you measure inflation matters enormously for assessing worker welfare. The standard CPI measures the change in the price of a fixed basket of goods. But workers' consumption patterns change in response to prices — substituting chicken for beef when beef becomes expensive — which means the CPI may overstate the true cost of living increases.

There's also the question of which price index to use. The CPI-W, calculated for urban wage earners, differs from the chained CPI, which accounts for substitution. The personal consumption expenditure deflator, preferred by the Federal Reserve, runs somewhat lower than the CPI.

These are not just academic debates. Social Security COLAs are tied to the CPI. Minimum wage proposals are often indexed to inflation. The choice of index has real distributional consequences.

## What the Episode Taught Us

The post-pandemic inflation episode, uncomfortable as it was, offered some useful lessons. It demonstrated that tight labor markets can generate real benefits for workers at the bottom of the distribution. It showed that the conventional assumption — that inflation always hurts workers and benefits capital — is too simple. And it highlighted the limits of our understanding of how wages and prices interact in conditions of rapid labor market change.

As inflation has receded and labor market conditions have normalized, the challenge is to preserve the gains at the bottom of the wage distribution while avoiding a return to the pre-pandemic pattern of stagnant wages and growing inequality.
