For decades, the minimum wage debate was shaped by a simple economic model: if you raise the price of labor above its market-clearing level, you will reduce employment. The prediction seemed clean and intuitive, and it generated a strong prior among economists against minimum wage increases.

Then Card and Krueger published their famous 1994 study of fast food employment in New Jersey and Pennsylvania, and the debate has never been the same.

## The Card-Krueger Revolution

Card and Krueger compared employment at fast food restaurants on both sides of the New Jersey-Pennsylvania border after New Jersey raised its minimum wage in 1992. If the standard model were right, employment should have fallen in New Jersey relative to Pennsylvania. It didn't. If anything, employment rose slightly.

The study was methodologically innovative — using the state border as a natural experiment to isolate the effect of the wage increase — and the findings were explosive. The economics profession pushed back hard. Critics questioned the data, the methodology, and the interpretation. But the result has held up. Subsequent researchers using similar border-discontinuity designs have repeatedly found that moderate minimum wage increases have small or no effects on employment.

## The Modern Evidence Base

The research has evolved substantially since 1994. We now have natural experiments from dozens of state and local minimum wage increases, large administrative datasets that allow researchers to track individual workers and businesses over time, and increasingly sophisticated methods for constructing counterfactuals.

The emerging consensus — to the extent there is one — is roughly as follows:

**Employment effects are small at moderate levels.** For minimum wages in the range of 50-60% of the local median wage, the employment effects are typically small, negative, and often indistinguishable from zero. This is broadly consistent with the findings of Dube, Lester, and Reich, whose influential work using border-county pairs became the methodological standard for the field.

**Hours and scheduling matter.** Some studies that find no effect on employment do find reductions in hours worked, particularly among affected workers. The distinction matters for worker welfare — a small cut in hours can meaningfully reduce income even when the hourly wage rises.

**Effects may differ at higher levels.** There is more uncertainty about minimum wages at very high levels — approaching 100% of the median wage or above. Some studies of Seattle's $15 minimum wage found larger employment and hours effects, particularly in low-wage industries. The literature here is genuinely contested.

## Why Simple Models Failed

Why did the standard competitive model get this so wrong? Several mechanisms help explain the divergence between theory and evidence.

First, labor markets have monopsonistic features that simple supply-and-demand models ignore. When employers have market power over workers — because workers face search frictions, switching costs, and imperfect information — wages can be held below competitive levels, and moderate wage floors can actually increase both wages and employment.

Second, higher wages may increase productivity through reduced turnover, higher effort, and better matching. If the efficiency wage hypothesis is right, the labor-demand curve doesn't simply slope downward in response to wage increases.

Third, minimum wage workers are primarily spending their income locally. Higher wages at the bottom can boost consumer spending in ways that support local employment, partially or fully offsetting any negative employment effects.

## What This Means for Policy

The empirical evidence has meaningfully shifted the policy debate. Most economists now support gradual increases in the federal minimum wage, which has been stuck at $7.25 since 2009. Many support more substantial increases to $15 or beyond.

But the research also counsels some caution. The effects of minimum wage increases vary by local labor market conditions. A $15 federal minimum wage that makes sense for Seattle or Boston may have larger negative effects in lower-wage regions of the country. Indexing the minimum wage to local median wages, rather than setting a single national floor, may better balance the competing considerations.

The debate is not over. But two decades of research have made the case for a more generous wage floor substantially stronger than it once appeared.
