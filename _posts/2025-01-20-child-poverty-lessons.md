For one year — 2021 — the United States ran a genuine experiment in child poverty reduction. The American Rescue Plan expanded the Child Tax Credit, made it fully refundable, and delivered monthly payments to nearly all families with children. The results were striking: child poverty fell to its lowest level ever recorded, by more than half.

Then it expired. Child poverty shot back up.

The episode offers a window into both the possibilities and the politics of social policy in America.

## What the Expansion Did

The original Child Tax Credit, established in 1997 and expanded several times since, provided up to $2,000 per child per year to families with sufficient earned income. The catch was in that phrase: *sufficient earned income.* Families at the very bottom — those with the lowest incomes or no formal employment — received little or nothing. The credit was designed to reward work, not to reduce poverty among the poorest children.

The 2021 expansion changed this in three key ways. It raised the maximum credit to $3,000 per child ($3,600 for children under six). It made the credit fully refundable, meaning even families with no income could receive the full amount. And it switched from annual tax refunds to monthly payments, putting cash in families' hands throughout the year rather than in a lump sum at tax time.

The monthly payments were particularly significant. Research on consumption smoothing shows that families — especially low-income families — benefit substantially from receiving predictable income flows rather than irregular windfalls.

## The Results

The evidence is clear and consistent across multiple data sources. Columbia University's Center on Poverty and Social Policy estimated that the expanded credit reduced child poverty by 30-40% during the months it was in effect. The official supplemental poverty measure showed child poverty falling from 9.7% in 2020 to 5.2% in 2021 — an extraordinary decline.

Food insufficiency among families with children fell sharply. Financial stress indicators improved. Parents reported better mental health. There was little evidence of the predicted negative effects on work incentives, though this remains a contested point in the literature.

## Why It Expired

The expanded credit lapsed at the end of 2021 when Congress failed to pass the Build Back Better legislation that would have extended it. The politics were complicated, but the key obstacle was a senator's concern about the impact on work incentives — an empirical claim that ran against the evidence but proved decisive in a 50-50 Senate.

This episode illustrates a persistent feature of American social policy: the tension between universalism and conditionality. Programs that require work or earnings as a condition of receipt enjoy broader political support but exclude the most vulnerable families. Programs that provide benefits regardless of employment status are more effective at reducing deep poverty but face stronger opposition.

## Lessons for Future Policy

The Child Tax Credit expansion demonstrates that child poverty in the United States is not an intractable problem. It is largely a policy choice. The cost of making the expanded credit permanent — roughly $100-120 billion per year — is substantial but not prohibitive, particularly given the long-run economic and social returns to reducing childhood poverty.

The research on the long-run effects of childhood poverty reduction is compelling. Children who receive more resources early in life have better educational outcomes, higher adult earnings, and better health. The investment pays for itself many times over.

What's missing is not evidence or even, arguably, the resources. What's missing is the political will to treat the elimination of child poverty as a national priority.
