In January 1937, the Ohio River crested at nearly sixty feet above flood stage. The water swallowed Louisville, Kentucky, displacing more than 230,000 people — one of the worst natural disasters in American history. Margaret Bourke-White, a photographer for *LIFE* magazine, was there.

The photograph she made on that assignment became one of the most reproduced images of the twentieth century. It shows a long line of Black flood victims waiting for food and relief supplies. Behind them, stretching across the full width of the frame, is a billboard: *World's Highest Standard of Living. There's no way like the American Way.* The billboard shows a cheerful white family — father, mother, children, a dog — riding in a gleaming automobile, smiling at the viewer.

The juxtaposition requires no caption.

## What the Photograph Shows

Bourke-White did not manufacture the irony. She found it. The billboard was a real National Association of Manufacturers campaign, part of a broader public relations offensive designed to counter the New Deal's implicit critique of American capitalism. The flood was real. The line was real.

What she captured was the gap between the advertisement's promise and the material reality facing Black Americans in the Jim Crow South. The people in the line were not outside the American economy — they were deeply inside it, as laborers, domestic workers, sharecroppers, factory hands. They had built much of the wealth that underwrote the "highest standard of living." They were simply excluded from its fruits.

## Disaster and Inequality

Natural disasters do not treat people equally. This was true in 1937 and it remains true today. The mechanisms of inequality that determine where people live, what resources they can draw on in a crisis, and how quickly they can recover are the same mechanisms that operate in ordinary times — disaster simply makes them visible.

The Louisville flood struck a city already stratified by race and class. Black residents were more likely to live in low-lying areas susceptible to flooding, had less access to private transportation to evacuate, and were more dependent on public relief. The relief system itself was racially segregated, as was most of American public life.

Bourke-White's photograph became famous in part because it made these abstractions concrete. You could not look at that line and at that billboard and pretend the contradiction did not exist.

## The Persistence of the Gap

Nearly ninety years later, the pattern Bourke-White documented persists. Study after study of contemporary natural disasters — Hurricane Katrina, Hurricane Maria, the Texas winter storm of 2021 — finds that low-income residents and communities of color suffer greater losses, receive less assistance, and recover more slowly. The mechanisms are different in their particulars but similar in their structure: differential exposure, differential resources, differential treatment by institutions.

The billboard has changed its slogans. The economy has grown enormously. The legal architecture of Jim Crow has been dismantled. But the underlying question the photograph poses — *for whom is the standard of living highest, and at whose expense?* — remains stubbornly, urgently relevant.

That is what makes this image, made in the mud of a flooded Kentucky city eighty-seven years ago, feel so contemporary. It is not a relic. It is a mirror.
