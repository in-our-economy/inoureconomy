The United States is in the grip of a housing affordability crisis that has been decades in the making. In city after city, rents have soared, homeownership has become a distant dream for younger Americans, and homelessness has surged even in periods of economic growth. But unlike many economic crises, the proximate causes of the housing shortage are reasonably well understood. The puzzle is political, not analytical.

## Supply Is the Core Problem

Economists across the ideological spectrum agree on the basic diagnosis: in the high-demand metropolitan areas where most economic opportunity is concentrated, housing supply has failed to keep pace with demand. The reasons are not mysterious. Local zoning laws in most American cities effectively ban the construction of apartment buildings on the vast majority of residential land. Single-family zoning, parking minimums, setback requirements, height limits, and lengthy permitting processes all combine to restrict the supply of new housing far below what a well-functioning market would produce.

The evidence for this supply-side story is compelling. Cities and states that have reformed their zoning laws — Minneapolis, Auckland, Tokyo — have seen more moderate rent growth than comparable places that have not. Studies of specific upzoning episodes consistently find that allowing more housing construction reduces rents in the surrounding area.

## The Political Economy of Scarcity

If supply is the problem and supply-side reforms are the solution, why has it taken so long for American cities to act? The answer lies in the political economy of housing. Existing homeowners, who tend to vote at higher rates than renters, have strong financial incentives to restrict new supply. Their homes are their primary financial assets, and in a constrained market, rising home prices represent a windfall.

This creates a troubling dynamic: the people most empowered to shape local land-use decisions are precisely those with the most to gain from restrictive zoning. Local planning meetings are dominated by homeowners who object to change in their neighborhoods, while the renters and would-be residents who would benefit from new construction are diffuse, unorganized, and often not yet present in the community.

> The exclusion of housing opponents is self-reinforcing: the people who would live in housing that doesn't get built can't show up to advocate for it.

## What Reform Looks Like

The good news is that the political environment is shifting. State legislatures in California, Oregon, Montana, and elsewhere have begun overriding local zoning restrictions, allowing greater density by right across wide swaths of residential land. The Biden administration made federal housing supply one of its signature economic policy priorities.

These reforms are necessary but not sufficient. Even in cities that have legalized more density, other barriers — construction costs, financing constraints, community opposition to specific projects — slow the pace of new construction. A comprehensive approach to housing affordability requires not just zoning reform but also investments in public and subsidized housing, reforms to the construction industry, and policies to address the financial speculation that drives up land costs.

## The Stakes

The housing crisis is not just an inconvenience for renters or a financial frustration for aspiring homeowners. It is a major driver of economic inequality, geographic immobility, and the long-run decline in economic dynamism. When workers cannot afford to live near the jobs that best match their skills, the entire economy becomes less productive. When families cannot put down roots in stable communities, the social fabric frays.

Getting housing policy right matters enormously — and the path forward, while politically difficult, is reasonably clear.
