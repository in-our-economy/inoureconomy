Wealth inequality in the United States is staggering. But measuring it precisely is surprisingly difficult — and the debates about measurement have real implications for how we understand the problem and what policies might address it.

## The Basic Picture

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, the most comprehensive survey-based measure of household wealth in the United States, shows the top 1% of families owning roughly 30-35% of total household wealth. The top 10% own around 65-70%. The bottom 50% own less than 3%.

These numbers are striking. But they may actually *understate* the true concentration of wealth at the very top. Survey-based measures have well-documented problems with capturing the holdings of the ultra-wealthy, who often decline to participate in surveys and whose assets are held in complex legal structures that are difficult to value.

## The Capital Income Approach

One alternative approach, developed by economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, uses tax data to infer wealth from capital income. The idea is simple: if you observe someone receiving $1 million in dividend income, and the dividend yield on stocks is 2%, that person likely holds around $50 million in stocks. By applying capitalization rates to different categories of capital income, researchers can reconstruct the distribution of wealth.

Using this approach, Saez and Zucman find even higher concentration at the top — with the top 0.1% owning roughly 20% of all wealth, and the wealth share of the very top continuing to rise.

## The Debates

The capitalization approach has critics. Matthew Smith, Danny Yagan, Owen Zidar, and Eric Zwick argue that Saez-Zucman's method overstates concentration by assuming that wealthy and less wealthy investors earn the same returns on similar assets. In practice, business owners — many of whom are not billionaires — earn very high returns on their equity, which gets attributed to wealth holdings that are actually more modest.

These debates may seem arcane, but they have policy implications. If wealth concentration is somewhat lower than Saez-Zucman suggest, the case for a wealth tax is somewhat different. If the very top is growing faster than the rest, the urgency of addressing concentration is greater.

## What We Know With Confidence

Despite these measurement debates, several things are clear:

Wealth is far more concentrated than income. Even the income distribution, which shows substantial inequality, looks relatively equal compared to the wealth distribution.

Racial wealth gaps are enormous. The median white family holds roughly eight times more wealth than the median Black family and five times more than the median Hispanic family. These gaps compound over generations and cannot be explained by income differences alone.

The top of the distribution has pulled away from the rest since the 1980s. Whatever the exact numbers, the trend toward greater concentration at the top is robust across data sources and methodologies.

## Why Measurement Matters

Getting the measurement right is not just an academic exercise. Tax policy, transfer policy, and regulatory choices all benefit from a clear picture of how wealth is distributed and how that distribution is changing.

The good news is that measurement is improving. Better access to administrative tax data, new approaches to valuing private businesses, and expanded wealth surveys are all helping to refine our understanding. The picture that emerges — even with uncertainty about the precise numbers — is one that should give policymakers serious pause.
