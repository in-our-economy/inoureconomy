The American Dream has always been partly a geographical promise — that anyone, regardless of birth, could rise. Research over the past decade has given us the most granular picture ever produced of how well that promise holds across different places. The results are sobering, but not without hope.

## The Chetty Revolution

Raj Chetty and his collaborators at Opportunity Insights have used administrative tax data linking parents and children to produce detailed maps of intergenerational mobility at the county, commuting zone, and even neighborhood level. This work — unprecedented in its scale and granularity — has transformed the debate about opportunity in America.

The core finding: where you grow up matters enormously for your adult outcomes, even holding constant the income level of your parents. Children from low-income families who grow up in certain places — parts of the upper Midwest, the Great Plains, many college towns — are substantially more likely to reach the middle class than comparable children who grow up in other places — the Deep South, parts of Appalachia, many large cities in the Northeast and Midwest.

## What Makes Places Different

Identifying the fact of geographic variation is one thing. Understanding what causes it is another. Chetty and colleagues have investigated a long list of potential explanations, and several factors emerge as consistently associated with better mobility.

**School quality** matters, but the relationship is complex. It's not just spending; it's the quality of instruction, peer effects, and the match between what schools teach and what local labor markets demand.

**Social capital and community ties** are strongly predictive. Places with more civic engagement, more trust, and denser social networks tend to produce better outcomes for children from all backgrounds.

**Income segregation** is a powerful negative predictor. When low-income and high-income families live in separate neighborhoods, children from low-income families lose access to the networks, role models, and resources available to their more affluent peers.

**Two-parent family structures** are statistically predictive of mobility at the community level — though this relationship is almost certainly driven by common underlying factors rather than reflecting a simple causal effect of family structure per se.

## The Neighborhood Effects

Perhaps the most striking result from Chetty's work concerns the causal effect of neighborhoods. Using the Moving to Opportunity experiment and other quasi-experimental evidence, he finds that moving to a higher-opportunity neighborhood during childhood — particularly before age 13 — has large positive effects on adult earnings and other outcomes.

Every year a child spends in a better neighborhood appears to improve their adult outcomes. The effects are not just about avoiding bad environments; they reflect the positive influence of better schools, peers, community norms, and networks.

## Implications for Policy

These findings suggest several policy priorities. Programs that enable low-income families to move to higher-opportunity neighborhoods — expanded housing vouchers, inclusionary zoning requirements that produce affordable units in high-opportunity areas — can have substantial long-run payoffs.

But place-based policies that try to improve low-opportunity areas also deserve attention. Investments in early childhood programs, K-12 education, and community development in lagging places have evidence behind them, even if the evidence on what specifically works is thinner.

The harder challenge is the political economy. High-opportunity neighborhoods often resist policies that would increase economic diversity. Improving low-opportunity places requires sustained long-run investment that typically exceeds political time horizons.

Understanding the geography of opportunity is the first step. Building the political will to act on that understanding is another challenge entirely.
