For decades, the story of American education economics was simple: go to college, earn more. The college wage premium — the gap between what college graduates and high school graduates earn — grew steadily from the 1970s through the 2000s, and became the primary justification for encouraging ever more Americans to pursue four-year degrees.

Recent data complicate this picture in interesting ways.

## The Premium Is Still Large, But Growth Has Stalled

The college wage premium remains substantial. College graduates earn, on average, roughly 65-70% more than high school graduates. Over a working lifetime, this translates to hundreds of thousands of dollars in additional earnings.

But the growth of the premium has slowed markedly since the early 2000s. Some researchers find that the premium has actually declined slightly for recent cohorts of graduates. And when you account for the rising cost of college — both tuition and foregone earnings — the return on investment from a four-year degree looks considerably different than it did when tuition was much lower.

## The Heterogeneity Problem

Averages are misleading here. The return to a college degree varies enormously depending on the field of study, the selectivity of the institution, and labor market conditions at graduation.

Graduates in engineering, computer science, nursing, and accounting typically see very high returns. Graduates in certain humanities and social science fields see more modest average earnings, though the variance is high and the non-monetary benefits of a broad education are real.

The institution matters too. A degree from an elite research university carries different labor market value than a degree from a less-selective regional institution. Yet the cost may not differ as much as the earnings premium would suggest.

## The Credential Problem

One explanation for stalling returns is credential inflation. As more workers obtain bachelor's degrees, employers require credentials for jobs that previously did not demand them. This raises the cost of not having a degree — not because the underlying skills taught in college have become more valuable, but because the credential has become a sorting mechanism.

If credential inflation is a significant driver of college attendance, it suggests a collective action problem: individuals are rational to attend college, but socially, we may be over-investing in credentials relative to skills.

## What This Means for Policy

The policy implications are significant. If the college premium is increasingly driven by credential signaling rather than skill acquisition, policies focused on expanding access to four-year degrees may be misallocating resources.

Several alternative directions deserve more attention: investing in high-quality vocational and apprenticeship programs that provide pathways to good jobs without requiring four years and $50,000+ in debt; reforming general education requirements so that students develop genuine analytical and communication skills rather than accumulating credits; and addressing the credential inflation problem directly by encouraging employers to move away from degree requirements where they are not genuinely predictive of job performance.

None of this means that college is not valuable — for many people and many purposes, it clearly is. But policy built on a simplified story of the college premium may be missing a more complicated and more actionable picture.
